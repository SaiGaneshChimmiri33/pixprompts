How to keep your face accurate in AI photo edits

Why faces change in AI edits, and the simple habits that keep the person in your photo looking like themselves.

Last updated: November 22, 2026

Almost everyone who tries AI photo editing has had the same moment: the style looks great, but the face in the picture is not quite yours. The nose is a little different, the jaw is softer, the eyes look like someone else’s. It is frustrating, but it is not random. Once you understand why it happens, you can prevent most of it.

Why AI changes faces in the first place

Many image tools do not simply paint over your picture. They rebuild it based on what they think the final image should look like. When your request is dramatic, for example “make me look like a movie poster in a snowy forest”, the tool pays more attention to the new scene and less attention to the exact details of your face. Blurry or dark source photos make this worse, because the tool has little reliable information to hold on to.

That is why the same prompt can give a perfect result on one photo and a stranger’s face on another. The difference is usually the source photo and how much you asked the tool to change.

Step 1: Begin with a strong source photo

Use a sharp, evenly lit picture where your face is clearly visible and mostly front-facing. Avoid photos with sunglasses, heavy filters or strong shadows across the face. If you only have a low-quality picture, do not expect the AI to recover features it cannot see. Our guide on preparing your photo covers this in detail.

Step 2: Tell the AI what to protect

Do not assume the tool knows that your face is the most important part. Say it. Useful details to name are face shape, eyes, nose, hairstyle, skin tone, pose and clothing. The prompts on PixPrompts end with a protective sentence for this reason. You can write your own version too:

Keep my face, features, hairstyle and skin tone exactly the same as in the original photo. Only change the background and lighting.

Notice that the sentence also says what can change. Giving the tool a clear permission and a clear limit works better than a vague instruction to “keep it realistic”.

Step 3: Change one big thing at a time

This is the habit that helps the most. If you ask for a new outfit, a new background, a new lighting style and a new art style in one go, the face usually pays the price. Instead, make the largest change first. When you are happy with it, run a second, smaller edit on the result. Two gentle steps are more accurate than one dramatic one.

Step 4: Compare carefully with the original

Place the original and the edited picture side by side and look at a few specific things:

  • Eye shape and distance between the eyes
  • Nose width and the shape of the tip
  • Jawline and chin
  • Hairline and parting
  • Any moles, dimples or scars that make you recognisable

If one of these has drifted, mention it directly in the next prompt, for example “keep my original hairline and the small mole on my left cheek”. Specific corrections usually work better than repeating the whole prompt.

Be careful with strong styles

Anime, cartoon, oil painting and fantasy styles are designed to simplify faces, so some change is expected. If a close likeness matters, choose realistic styles such as cinematic, studio portrait, vintage film or natural light. If you do want a stylised look, ask for “a recognisable likeness of the person in the photo” and review the result honestly.

Avoid over-retouching

Words like “flawless”, “perfect skin” and “beautiful” push many tools to smooth and reshape faces. Phrases such as “natural skin texture” and “minimal retouching” keep you looking like yourself. Remember that a realistic photo of you with a good background is usually more satisfying than a perfect face that no longer looks like you.

When it still goes wrong

Try the same prompt a couple of times, because results vary between attempts. If every attempt drifts, simplify the prompt and try a different, better-lit photo. Different AI tools also behave differently, so it can help to test the same prompt in more than one. Finally, keep a record of what worked, so next time you can reuse the wording that preserved your features.

Always use photos you have the right to edit. If a picture shows another person, make sure they are happy for you to edit it. For help with the rest of the edit, see how to fix common AI photo editing mistakes.
